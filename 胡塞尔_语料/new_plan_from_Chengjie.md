Leib and Leibkörper:  
On the Constitution and Genesis of Leiblichkeit in Husserl’s Phenomenology

1\. Problem Statement and Research Questions

Within the transcendental phenomenology of Husserl, one of the most challenging tasks is to adequately and precisely characterize “the correlation a priori of intentionality”, particularly on the noetic side. The “constitutor” can neither be understood as some kind of “world-creating substance” nor as a “higher animal” in the sense of the natural sciences. This leaves relatively few resources available from the preceding philosophical tradition. The problem of the body therefore gradually becomes an important issue for Husserl’s attempt to characterize concrete subjectivity and its constitutive functions.

The most familiar example is Husserl’s detailed investigation and differentiation of the body in *Ideas II*. Put roughly, the Leib/Körper distinction not only discloses a distinction between the constituting subject as embodied and all constitutive objects, including physical things; it also reveals a twofold character of the body conception itself: the body as a perceiving subject and the body as an object perceived.[^1] Its significance therefore could not be overstate, especially because it shows possibility of **a concrete determination** of the noesis/noema structure of intentionality. Yet if we focus exclusively on the Leib/Körper distinction, two broad questions inevitably arise: (1) How can Husserl legitimately introduce the concept of Leib, which is bound up with the concept of the Ich? (2) If the noetic side is understood as the side of the constituting subject, is the concept of the Leib sufficient to fully describe that subject?[^2]

I do not intend to answer these two questions directly. Instead, it first addresses a more specific problem related to them: the Leib revealed by the case of “double touch” has a **twofold roles**, insofar as it is simultaneously constituting and constituted. At the same time, however, this Leib seems to encompass several determinations ranging from the formal to the concrete—for example, both the zero-point (Nullpunkt) of spatial orientation and the unity that carries the kinaesthetic system. Can such a Leib avoid ambiguity or even contradiction in specific phenomenological analysis? How should the different levels within one and the same concept be understood, and is a foundational relation between these levels possible? Interestingly, in *Ideas I*, published in the same period, Husserl’s most familiar concept of the pure ego (das reine Ich) faces a structurally similar difficulty: on the one hand, Husserl repeatedly describes the pure ego as a “pole” (Pol) with “no explicable content”; on the other hand, this pure ego must nevertheless function as a subject in concrete contexts such as value judgments. Husserl himself reflects on this difficulty in his later work.[^3] Although, beginning in Ideas II, he increasingly introduces a concrete subject endowed with personality, habituality, and related determinations, and gradually develops the concept of the “monad”, the monad thereby exceeds the pure ego and forms a new conceptual level. Might Leib likewise involve a tension in its determination, leading to a comparable kind of internal differentiation? The relation between the pure ego and the monad seems to provide a useful point of comparison.

Moreover, the Leib/Körper distinction receives much attention in Ideas II, although the two are not in fact sharply separated. In this work Husserl alternates among terms such as Leibkörper[^4] and leiblicher Körper, and later also uses Körperleib and körperlicher Leib. Leibkörper, in particular, becomes a concept used in later analyses of intersubjectivity and genetic phenomenology.[^5] These intermediate expressions not only blur the Leib/Körper distinction; it also intensifies the tension described above. In several contexts, Leibkörper appears to assume functions that had originally been assigned to Leib, especially functions associated with its status as constituted. If this tension cannot simply be dissolved, and if Leib must therefore be differentiated internally, we are once again led to the two broader questions: (1) Where does the “formal Leib” located on the constitutive side come from, and can it be understood independently of the constituted side? (2) Whether the constituting Leib is sufficient to fully describe the subject?

These two questions in turn concern a more fundamental issue: how should we understand “Leib as a **constitutive condition (of the subject)**”? When we say that the constitution of external or transcendent objects depends upon the body[^6], which level or levels of the multilayered Leib identified above should count as the condition? And, as a condition, should Leib be understood empirically or transcendentally? If it is understood transcendentally, would such a conception conflict with our broader understanding of the Husserlian “transcendental”? This question becomes particularly significant in light of Kant’s late manuscripts, in which embodiment is also considered in connection with the conditions of the possibility of experience.[^7] The body may therefore provide a productive entry point for reconsidering the relation between the concepts of the transcendental in Kant and Husserl.

2.  State of Research

    2.1 Leib/Körper and Husserl’s Phenomenology of the Body

    Research on Husserl’s phenomenology of the body has long centered on the Leib/Körper distinction. Studies have examined the body in its double significance as lived body and physical object, as well as the relation between this duality and perception, kinaesthesia, the constitution of space, empathy, and intersubjectivity. Behnke’s study of Ideas II was among the earlier systematic accounts of the significance of Husserl’s analysis of the body within his phenomenological system[^8]; Dodd subsequently situated the problem of the body within the broader context of Husserl’s transcendental idealism and the relation between subject and world.[^9] Research has since focused increasingly on kinaesthesia, and the body as a condition of perception and action. Ferencz-Flatz provides a systematic reconstruction of the development of Husserl’s concept of kinaesthesia[^10]; Heinämaa, meanwhile, reconsidered the constitutive significance of Leib by examining the relation between bodily movement, touch, and transcendental phenomenology.[^11] Wehrle further analyzes the twofold temporality of the body and the distinction between “being a body” and “having a body.”[^12] Existing research has thus provided a substantial account of how the Leib/Körper distinction concretizes the bodily structure of subjectivity and intentionality. At the same time, whether the Leib/Körper distinction is sufficient to exhaust Husserl’s actual use of bodily concepts remains an open question.

    2.2 Leibkörper and a Reconsideration of the Leib/Körper Distinction

    Recent research has begun to question directly whether Leib/Körper should be regarded as the final binary structure of Husserl’s theory of the body, and has instead returned to the theoretical status of Leibkörper. Didier Franck was an early and influential figure in this development. Taking intersubjectivity and the Fifth Cartesian Meditation as his point of departure, he argues that the incarnation presupposed by the constitution of the Other—whereby my Leib acquires the significance of a Körper, i.e. becomes a Leibkörper—cannot be completed within the primordial sphere by appeal to a supposedly pure “Leib”.[^13] Battán Horenstein has devoted a specific analysis to Leib, Körper, and Leibkörper in *Ideas II* and argues that Leibkörper has a significance that cannot be adequately explained by the simple Leib/Körper distinction.[^14] Liu’s recent study likewise argues that Husserl’s concepts of the body cannot be adequately explained by either the Leib/Körper distinction or the “inner-body/outer-body” distinction alone, and emphasizes the more complex relation among Leib, Körper, and Leibkörper.[^15] Thus, recent scholarship has begun to shift from Leib/Körper toward Leibkörper. Yet existing studies have focused primarily on the semantic and experiential significance of Leibkörper, while paying less systematic attention to the changing relation between the terms Leib and Leibkörper across Husserl’s development from static to genetic phenomenology.

    2.3 The Constitutive Function of Leib and the Conditionality of the Body

    A further major line of research concerns the functions assumed by Leib in specific constitutive activities, particularly Nullpunkt, Kinästhesie, touch, spatial orientation, and the constitution of objects. Existing studies show that Leib does not appear merely as a given bodily object, but also participates in the constitution of object-appearance and subject-experience. Heinämaa discusses the body as a condition of experience from the perspective of transcendental phenomenology[^16]; Slatman, from the perspective of bodily habituality and bodily change, analyzes Leib as a bearer of capacities for action and bodily experience.[^17] Research has therefore provided a relatively detailed account of the constitutive functions that Leib can perform. A further question nevertheless remains: when we say that Leib is a constitutive condition, which level of Leib are we referring to? What is the relation between Leib as Nullpunkt, Kinästhesieträger, and Empfindungsträger, on the one hand, and Leibkörper as a concrete bodily unity, on the other?

    2.4 From Static to Genetic Phenomenology: The Formation of Concrete Subjectivity

    Research on Husserl’s transition from static to genetic phenomenology offers another important clue for reconsidering these questions. Donohoe emphasizes that genetic phenomenology transforms subjectivity from a merely formal pole within static constitution into a concrete subject with habituality, history, and processes of becoming.[^18] Marosan likewise emphasizes the stratification between the pure ego and concrete subjectivity.[^19] In this connection, Husserl’s analyses of the relation between the pure ego (reines Ich) and the monad have received considerable attention. Mertens examines the concept of the monad in relation to Husserl’s engagement with Leibniz, while studies in genetic phenomenology emphasize the concreteness of the monad in comparison with the pure ego, including its habituality, history, and relation to the world.[^20] Yet research on the reines Ich–Monade relation and research on the Leib–Leibkörper relation have largely remained separate. This raises a further question: might these two relations display a structural correspondence, such that reines Ich/Monade and Leib/Leibkörper could serve as mutually illuminating structures for understanding Husserl’s movement from formal poles toward concrete subjectivity? The crucial point is not to presuppose an isomorphism, but to investigate whether Leib likewise exhibits a movement from a formal constitutive pole toward a concrete bodily unity.

    2.5 The Body and Transcendental Subjectivity: Transzendentale Leiblichkeit

    Finally, directly relevant to the present problem is the literature on the relation between embodiment/Leiblichkeit and transcendentality. Dodd situated the problem of the body within Husserl’s transcendental idealism[^21]; De Santis explicitly discusses the relation between Leib and transcendental idealism and argues that embodied subjectivity need not conflict with Husserl’s transcendental position.[^22] Heinämaa and others further examine the relation among transcendental subjectivity, embodiment, and genesis, showing that the body cannot simply be assigned to the level of empirical psychology, but can participate in the constitution of transcendental subjectivity.[^23] This body of research gives transzendentale Leiblichkeit a clear theoretical significance, while also indicating that the transcendental status of the body cannot be understood independently of its concrete constitutive functions and its relation to subjectivity. The relation among Leib, Leibkörper, and transcendental subjectivity therefore remains in need of further clarification.

3.  Research Gap

    Existing research has illuminated the basic structures of Husserl’s phenomenology of the body from several perspectives. Classical studies centered on the Leib/Körper distinction have clarified the double significance of the body as Leib and as Körper, as well as the relation of embodiment to perception, kinaesthesia, spatial orientation, and object constitution. More recent research on Leibkörper has further indicated that the simple Leib/Körper distinction does not exhaust Husserl’s actual analyses of bodily unity. At the same time, research on genetic phenomenology has shown that, in the transition from static to genetic phenomenology, Husserl’s conception of subjectivity increasingly involves habituality, capacities, historicity, and concrete psychosomatic unity. Research on the transcendental significance of the body has likewise raised the question of how the body can simultaneously belong to empirical subjectivity and participate in transcendental constitution.

    Although existing studies have separately examined the constitutive functions of Leib and the possible concrete unity signified by Leibkörper, the use of the term Leibkörper across Husserl’s writings and its interweaving with Leib have not yet been investigated systematically. The absence of this central nexus leaves these lines of research largely disconnected. Existing scholarship has shown what constitutive functions Leib can perform and has shown that Leibkörper cannot simply be reduced to Körper as a physical object. It has not, however, adequately answered a more fundamental question: when Leib is characterized both as a condition of constitutive activity and as possessing a constituted dimension, do these two determinations belong to the same level of embodiment, or do they point to different phenomenological unities? Does the distinction between Leib and Leibkörper therefore need to be reformulated within the framework of (genetic) phenomenology?

    This problem becomes particularly acute in Husserl’s transition from static to genetic phenomenology. In static constitutive analyses, Leib can function as zero-point, kinaesthetic organ, and bearer of sensations; in genetic phenomenology, however, the body (or bodily subject) increasingly appears as an unity characterized by habituality, capacities, tendencies toward action, and sedimented history. This raises a question that has not yet been treated systematically: is Leibkörper merely an already constituted bodily unity, or does it gradually become, with the development of genetic phenomenology, the concrete unity of an embodied subjectivity endowed with genetic structures? What is the relation between this concrete unity and the monad as a concrete transcendental subject? Correspondingly, does Leib remain merely the constitutive dimension within this unity, or does its own conceptual determination also undergo transformation?

    The problem can be further clarified through Husserl’s analyses of the self. Existing research on the pure ego and the monad has shown that, in Husserl’s development, an important structural tension emerges between the pure ego as a formal pole and the monad as a concrete subject with habituality, history, and a determinate mode of existence. This suggests an illuminating comparative question: is there a structural similarity between Leib and Leibkörper and the relation between the pure ego and the monad? If the pure ego, as a formal pole of subjectivity, is increasingly situated within the more concrete structure of the monad in genetic phenomenology, can Leib and Leibkörper be reconsidered in an analogous way? More precisely, might Leib in certain constitutive analyses appear as a formal constitutive pole of embodiment, while Leibkörper expresses a more concrete bodily unity endowed with “genetic” structures? This correspondence must not be treated as an identity or an equation already established by Husserl. It is first of all a structural hypothesis to be tested against the texts. Its significance lies in the possibility that it may provide an alternative framework for understanding Leib and Leibkörper beyond the traditional Leib/Körper dichotomy, and thereby explain why, in Husserl’s later genetic phenomenology, the determination of Leib becomes increasingly intertwined with concrete embodiment.

    This also gives rise to a further question concerning the “transcendental body” (transzendentaler Leib). If Leib is, on the one hand, a constituted body and, on the other, functions as zero-point, kinaesthetic system, bearer of sensations, and condition of object constitution, in what sense should it be called “transcendental”? The issue is not merely whether the body has transcendental significance, but first of all which form of embodiment performs the constitutive function, and what relation this constitutive embodiment bears to Leibkörper as a concrete bodily unity. The interpretation of transzendentaler Leib therefore cannot be separated from the internal relation between Leib and Leibkörper; it must be grounded in a renewed determination of their constitutive and genetic status.

    The research gap to be addressed here therefore does not consist primarily in proposing a previously unknown concept of the body, nor simply in demonstrating the importance of Leibkörper within Husserl’s philosophy of the body. Rather, the central gap is that existing research has not yet placed the Leib/Körper distinction, the problem of Leibkörper, the constitutive functions of Leib, and the concretization of subjectivity in static and genetic phenomenology within a single systematic framework, and has not thereby asked whether the relation between Leib and Leibkörper itself undergoes a structural transformation in the course of Husserl’s phenomenological development.

    The present study therefore takes this relation as its central problem. It examines the different determinations and functions of Leib and Leibkörper from *Ideas II* through Husserl’s later genetic phenomenological writings, and further tests whether their relation can be reconsidered through comparison with the structure of the pure ego and the monad. Through this investigation, the study seeks to determine the systematic position of embodiment between constitution and genesis, and thereby to reconsider in what sense the notion of a “transcendental body” can be sustained.

4.  Working Thesis and Research Objectives

    The basic hypothesis of this study is that Leib and Leibkörper are not two concepts that can be placed statically side by side within Husserl’s theory of the body. Rather, they may mark different determinations of embodiment along the dimensions of constitution and genesis, and their relation itself may be reconfigured as Husserl moves from static to genetic phenomenology, and what else is, the term Leibkörper itself experienced a change in use during Husserl’s different period.

    The traditional Leib/Körper distinction reveals the double significance of the body as subjective body and objective body, but it does not sufficiently explain the recurring appearance of Leibkörper in Husserl’s texts or the multiple functions assigned to Leib. The study therefore hypothesizes that the constitutive functions assigned to Leib cannot simply be understood as formal conditions of subjectivity separated from concrete embodiment. Rather, in the course of Husserl’s phenomenological development, Leib functions on the one hand as a constitutive condition through such determinations as Nullpunkt, Kinästhesieträger, and Empfindungsträger, while on the other hand becoming increasingly connected with genetic determinations such as Vermögen, Habitualität, Trieb, and Entwicklung. Correspondingly, Leibkörper should not be understood merely as an already constituted Körper, but must be examined as a possible bearer of these genetic determinations and as a concrete bodily unity.

    The central object of reconsideration is therefore not the isolated definition of Leib or Leibkörper, but the systematic relation between them. The study will test whether this relation can be understood as a development of embodiment from a formal constitutive dimension toward a concrete genetic unity, and whether this development can explain the functional differences assumed by Leib and Leibkörper at different stages of Husserl’s writings.

    On this basis, the study advances a further structural hypothesis: the relation between Leib and Leibkörper may correspond, in a structural sense, to the relation between the pure ego (reines Ich) and the monad (Monade). In the latter relation, the pure ego as a formal pole of subjectivity becomes increasingly situated within the concrete structure of the monad, which possesses habituality, history, and individuality. Correspondingly, can Leib be understood as a formal constitutive pole of embodiment, while Leibkörper represents the concrete unity of embodiment within genetic phenomenology? This correspondence is not presupposed as an isomorphism; it is a hypothesis to be tested through a historical-systematic analysis of Husserl’s texts.

    If this hypothesis is supported, the theoretical significance of Leibkörper must be reconsidered. Leibkörper would not merely be a supplementary term to the Leib/Körper distinction, but could constitute an important mediating structure for understanding the development of embodiment from constitution to genesis and from formal conditions toward concrete subjectivity. This would also allow the notion of transzendentale Leiblichkeit to be reformulated: the transcendental significance of the body should not be understood as the positing of an abstract “transcendental body” beyond the empirical body, but should be specified through the constitutive relation and genetic structure of Leib and Leibkörper.

    The aim of the study is therefore not to provide an isolated definition of Leib or Leibkörper, but to reconstruct their systematic relation within Husserl’s phenomenology and to show how this relation changes with the transition from static to genetic phenomenology. Through this reconstruction, the study seeks to clarify three interrelated questions: first, what phenomenological status should be assigned to the constitutive functions of Leib; second, how does Leibkörper move from being a constituted bodily unity into a structure of genetic subjectivity; and third, once this relation has been clarified, in what sense can the transcendental character of embodiment be legitimately articulated?
